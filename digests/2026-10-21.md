**How a Frontier Team Can Help Your Company Keep Up With AI (AI & I by Every)**
The Takeaway: The way to stop an AI-savvy company from chasing shiny objects is to split the job in two, a small team that lives on the frontier and everyone else who ships, with a deliberate membrane between them.

Every CEO Dan Shipper and head of enterprise AI adoption Natalia explain the structure they've been running for a couple of months. Every employee faces the same impossible choice: get your work done, or stay current on AI. Ignore the frontier and you risk being disrupted by an agent-native competitor; chase it and you never build anything durable. The frontier team resolves it by taking the people who love playing with new tools and unleashing them on experiments, whose low-end output is great content and whose high-end output gets pushed into real products. Shipper's key staffing insight is that you need both "pirates" and "architects": pirates sprint through a billion sloppy experiments in days, architects arrive just before handoff to compress the mess into something maintainable. As he puts it: "Most of that stuff gets thrown out. And some of it ends up getting pushed into the rest of the team." The rest of the episode is a tour of what individual members built, including head of evals Mike Taylor using Opus with hundreds of orchestrated agents to pull a narrative explainer video out of his own Slack messages and meeting notes, with every sentence traceable back to a source; senior editor Jack Cheng building a tabless browser to protect his attention; and engineer Jannik Jung running a multi-agent orchestrator that works at the level of goals and plans rather than individual coding sessions. Kieran Klaassen's closing advice for anyone starting a frontier team is the sharpest part: the job is integration, not experimentation. "If you do a hundred experiments and don't let it land and don't integrate it, it doesn't work." Dumping a hundred experiments into a channel just overwhelms the organization.
