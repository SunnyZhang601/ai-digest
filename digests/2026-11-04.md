**AI & I by Every — The Inside Story of the 50 Who Built Modern AI With Kevin Roose**
The Takeaway: The people who built modern AI are not doomsday cultists or pure hype merchants, and the future is not predestined, but the window for choosing how this goes is narrow and closing.

Kevin Roose spent a decade as a tech reporter at the New York Times and is now cohost of Machine Gods with Casey Newton. His new book, a New York Times bestseller, is an attempt to answer one question for himself: how did we actually get here? He interviewed roughly 150 people, including the 50 most central figures in the race to AGI, and came away neither reassured nor resigned. "I don't think we're doomed. But we have a lot to do."

What makes his account unusually specific is how he got it. Most interviews were on background, and the first 25 were the hardest, until the network effect kicked in: researchers started talking because a rival had. He also did the reading, showing up able to ask about specific papers, which is why the book contains artifacts like Dario Amodei's 2017 "big blob of compute hypothesis" memo, written before LLMs existed, arguing against narrow task-specific models and for pointing massive unstructured compute at general problems. Roose reads that memo as the moment the lightbulb went on about scaling.

On policy, he is blunt: federal regulation is not coming from the current administration unless something goes badly wrong, and crisis-driven legislation is a bad way to govern technology. His proposed floor is table stakes borrowed from pharma, mandatory safety testing before a model is released or deployed, to check it can't run autonomous cyberattacks or be weaponized for bioweapons. Beyond that, he has become convinced in recent months that a coordination mechanism to slow the frontier is now plausible, because leaders like Dario, Demis and Sam genuinely want to buy their safety teams time.

His most useful mental model for practitioners is a distinction between weightlifting tasks and forklift tasks. Weightlifting is where the struggle is the benefit, and bringing a forklift into the gym makes everyone weaker. Forklifting is moving something heavy from point A to point B, where refusing the machine is just waste. He weightlifts parenting and forklifts the legal and process grind of starting a company. He also describes a "council of Claudes" that reviewed his chapters as personas, an AI skeptic, an AI historian, a craft-focused editor, and notes the shift: "A couple years ago, we had to fact check the AIs, and now the AIs fact check me."

https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLuMcoKK9mKgHtW_o9h5sGO2vXrffKHwJL

核心要点：构建现代 AI 的这批人既不是末日教派，也不是纯粹的炒作贩子，未来并非注定，但选择走向的窗口很窄，而且在关闭。

Kevin Roose 在纽约时报做了十年科技记者，现在是和 Casey Newton 一起主持 Machine Gods 的主播。他的新书是纽约时报畅销书，目的很个人：搞清楚我们到底是怎么走到今天的。他采访了大约 150 人，其中包括 AGI 竞赛中最核心的 50 位人物，结论既不是安心，也不是认命。"我不认为我们注定完蛋，但我们有很多事要做。"

这本书之所以具体得罕见，在于他获取信息的方式。大部分采访都在 background 条件下进行，前 25 个最难，直到网络效应出现：研究者开始愿意开口，因为对手已经说了。他也做足了功课，能就具体论文提问，所以书里才有 Dario Amodei 2017 年那份"big blob of compute hypothesis"备忘录这样的材料。那份备忘录写在 LLM 出现之前，主张不要做狭窄的任务专用模型，而应该把大规模非结构化算力指向通用问题。Roose 认为那就是关于 scaling 的灯泡亮起的时刻。

谈到政策，他很直接：除非出了大事，现政府不会推出联邦层面的 AI 监管，而靠危机驱动立法是治理技术的糟糕方式。他提出的底线借鉴了制药业，模型发布或部署前必须做强制安全测试，确认它不会发起自主网络攻击、不会被武器化制造新型生物武器。在此之上，他最近几个月被说服了：现在有可能建立某种协调机制来放慢前沿，因为 Dario、Demis、Sam 这些领导者确实想给自家安全团队争取时间。

对从业者最有用的思维模型，是他对"举重任务"和"叉车任务"的区分。举重的意义在于挣扎本身，把叉车开进健身房只会让所有人更弱。叉车任务是把重物从 A 搬到 B，拒绝机器纯属浪费。他自己举重的是育儿，交给叉车的是创业过程中法务和流程上的琐碎消耗。他还描述了一个"Claude 议会"，用不同人格审阅他的章节：AI 怀疑论者、AI 历史学家、关注手艺的编辑，并点出了变化："几年前我们得给 AI 做事实核查，现在 AI 给我做事实核查。"
