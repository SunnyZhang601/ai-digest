**Latent Space: What Frontier Labs Buy: A Data Deep Dive with Handshake AI — Garrett Lord & Team**

The Takeaway: Frontier labs are no longer buying labels, they are buying simulated worlds authored by domain experts, and one unit of that data can take days to build.

Handshake AI's Garrett Lord, Nishant, Sahil and the science team lay out what the data business has actually become. Three years ago it was a billion-dollar market, now it is 10 billion, and Garrett does not see how it is not a hundred billion three years out. The old image of labeling, drawing boxes around cars at ten dollars an hour, is gone. What labs want now is RL environments: a realistic simulated bank or law firm or hospital with simulated Excel, Outlook, Bloomberg and SEC Edgar, populated with internally consistent data, a task prompt, and a rubric that codifies what good looks like, sometimes hundreds of rows long. Most knowledge work is not perfectly verifiable, so codifying expertise is the hard part.

The most counterintuitive claim is about headcount. As models get better, Handshake expects to hire far more full-time experts, not fewer: hundreds today, thousands later, people from elite finance and drug discovery backgrounds who manage and hire tastemakers, because data quality directly shapes the model's taste. Sahil notes that customers often use under ten percent of the data they procure, and that is not waste, it is the iterative search for the right data mix. Nishant, who spent five years on the lab side, describes how testing data itself became agentified, and how bad data can produce beautiful loss curves and a model that makes no sense.

The science section is the sharpest part. On Handshake's life sciences benchmark Vials, every model scores 50% or worse, with some under 20%, on tasks like counting hydrogen bonds in a GLP-1 binding diagram or reading a plasmid map. As Nick Magazine puts it: "AI is not gonna cure cancer before it can interpret these very basic types of images." A histology example is blunter: asked to find the two most diseased slides, Opus 5.5 picks G, H and I, and treating the patient in panel G would actively harm them. The failure is often in the vision encoder, which loses the salient detail on the way to a latent vector, which is why Handshake generates question-conditioned image descriptions as an auxiliary reward to test whether the encoder works at all.
