**Andrej Karpathy (OpenAI co-founder, independent AI researcher)**
Karpathy revisited an April tweet to argue the gap in shared understanding of LLM capability is *widening* into a sharp funnel. He breaks the world into four tiers: roughly 6B people (~75%) who have barely touched LLMs; 1-2B (~20%) casual free-tier users who see "derpy chatbots"; ~20M professionals (~0.2%) using frontier models in math and code who watch weeks-long projects collapse into a single prompt; and ~5,000 people (~0.00006%) with internal access to frontier systems, where swarms of thousands of agents run cyber attacks, discover new science, and push mathematics forward while human review falls behind. He attributes the funnel to three forces: impact scaling with problem ambition and horizon, the jaggedness of capability (peaks in digital, verifiable, economically valuable domains like math and code), and access tiers.

## PODCASTS

**No Priors — Frontier Chips for Frontier AI Labs, with Walter Goodwin, Founder/CEO of Fractile**
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OpeCP4wCxkA

The Takeaway: The winning chip company of the AI era won't be the one with the biggest FLOPS number, it'll be the one that can structurally carve out a three-to-six-month speed advantage over rivals and keep refreshing it.

Walter Goodwin runs Fractile, a full-stack AI chip company he started in summer 2022 to build very fast inference chips for the world's largest models. His core bet is speed, and specifically memory bandwidth, the rate at which a chip can pull model weights and KV cache out of memory. He argues most chips on the market today are "relatively identikit": same HBM memory, same Tensor Core bets, same TSMC advanced packaging, often designed in-house but delivered in partnership with a small number of ASIC houses like Broadcom. Fractile's differentiation is owning the entire stack, from architecture and front-end design down to physical design, packaging, and foundry interactions, with only about 150 people.

The company spent its first two years on an SRAM-based design (like Groq or Cerebras) but pivoted as context lengths grew, because SRAM is fast but tiny. The new platform, ramping in the second half of next year, aims to unite the scalability of cheap, high-capacity DRAM with the bandwidth of SRAM, targeting roughly 25x more bandwidth per chip than an HBM-based chip.

Goodwin's most counterintuitive point is that raw speed isn't about snappier chatbots. "The snappier chatbot is kind of the faster horses of fast inference," he says. The real prize is making long-running agents radically faster, and unlocking scaling laws for bandwidth, like sparser mixture-of-experts models that are currently too bandwidth-starved to serve efficiently. He also explains why frontier labs won't all roll their own silicon: going all-in on proprietary hardware is an asymmetric bet, since a rival's algorithmic breakthrough could obsolete your chip in the nine months before you deploy enough of it.

On timelines, he applies a simple heuristic: whenever someone gives an estimate, divide it by four. Asked about a top semiconductor CEO's ten-year prediction for going from architect intent to a finished GDSII file, he says divide by four and subtract a bit, though final sign-off via Cadence and Synopsys will persist for a long time. His closing argument: as compute becomes existential, every frontier player needs diversity of supply, and third-party chip makers survive because labs can't afford to bet the company on one proprietary design.
