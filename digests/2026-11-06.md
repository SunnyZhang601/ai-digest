**Latent Space — Controlling Biology - Fabian Theis, Helmholtz Munich**
https://www.youtube.com/@LatentSpacePod

The Takeaway: Computational biology has moved past mapping and prediction into a third phase, actively steering the next experiment, and the bottleneck is no longer model architecture but the discipline of building honest baselines and closed lab-in-the-loop cycles.

Fabian Theis runs the Computational Health Center at Helmholtz Munich, one of Europe's largest academic health AI efforts with roughly 45 group leaders and 500 to 600 people. His lab has been central to the virtual cell idea since 2019, and his tools like Scanpy and anndata became the foundation of scVerse, which most computational biologists have touched at some point. He is unusually candid about what has and hasn't worked.

His most useful correction to the field's hype is about scale versus baselines. When datasets were small and questions simple, fancy models had nothing to beat. Now that a single experiment can measure ten million transcriptomes, the picture changes, but he still tells students to keep the boring comparison. "I know you wanna do the most fancy deep learning thing, but please, please, you know, have the baseline." For simple tasks like cell type prediction, logistic regression remains close to state of the art. The famous Arc Institute virtual cell competition was, in his words, a glass of cold water in everyone's face, because fixed metrics could be gamed. The real winner, once exploitation was stripped out, was a flow matching approach, which tells you some nonlinearity is genuinely required for harder questions.

The deeper conceptual move is treating a perturbation model as a world model: not F of X but F of X comma A, state plus action predicting the next state. That requires separating representation learning from dynamics, which is why his lab pairs a contrastive representation model trained on 350 million cells across 16 species with a flow matching generative model on top. His newest work, Lab Compass, is an inverse generative model that runs the loop backwards: given a forward world model, tell the experimenter what to do to reach a desired cell state.

He is also refreshingly honest about commercialization. Platform companies that "decode biology" without saying what they sell tend to pivot to "we make a drug," because that is what investors can price. The sweet spot he sees is coupling modeling with proprietary data generation, such as the Cancer Organoid Perturbation Atlas consortium where pharma buys in for early access. And on talent, his Y model of dual advisors, one wet lab and one methods, has been running for eight years at the Munich School for Data Science, producing people who can translate a fuzzy biological question into a metric and a model.
